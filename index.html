Sizing a pneumatic launch, from compressor to catch car

How do you size the compressor, receiver tank, piston, and supply line for a compressed-air roller-coaster launch so a train reaches its target speed in the required time — without losing too much pressure to friction along the way? This site is the project: an interactive sizing demo, the full calculation, and the documentation of how it was built with AI.

In plain English

Some roller coasters don't roll down a big hill to get going. Instead, a machine yanks the train forward — really fast — like a slingshot. On Maxx Force, the S&S compressed-air coaster at Six Flags Great America, the slingshot is a giant blast of squeezed air. This project answers a simple question: how big does that air machine need to be? How hard a push, how much air per launch, how big a storage tank, and how wide a pipe — so the train hits 78 mph in under 3 seconds without wasting pressure along the way. Section 2 · The system

Store slowly, spend fast

A pneumatic launch (S&S's compressed-air design, used on Maxx Force) separates making power from delivering it. A modest compressor spends ~90 seconds charging a receiver tank; the launch then spends that stored air in under 3 seconds. That is why the peak mechanical power (3.46 MW) can be ~11× the compressor power (305 kW) — the central Transport 1 idea of this project. For contrast, Kingda Ka's Intamin launch did the same store-slowly-spend-fast trick with pressurized oil and hydraulic motors; the S&S design sized here does it with compressed air and a piston.

In plain English

Here's the trick, and it's the whole idea of the project: save up slowly, spend it all at once. A normal compressor isn't strong enough to launch a coaster by itself — that would take a gigantic, expensive machine. So instead, it spends about 90 seconds slowly squeezing air into a storage tank, like inflating a balloon a little at a time. Then — whoosh — the tank releases everything in under 3 seconds. That burst is about 11 times stronger than the compressor alone. It's the same reason you charge your phone for an hour and then it can flash its camera light in a split second: storing energy lets you spend it faster than you made it. Read the 7 boxes below left to right — that's the journey the air takes, from quiet tank to flying train.

Foundations — the balances every step draws on

Transport 1 is built from one bookkeeping statement applied three ways — to mass, to momentum, and to energy. The four blocks below state each balance in its general form, then show the reduced form this project actually uses and where it is used. Like the steps, they are symbolic only; every number waits for the worked example.

Foundation A — General balance equation & continuity (mass)

Every balance in this course has the same shape: accumulation = in − out + generation − consumption, written over a control volume. For mass there is no generation or consumption term, so the integral mass balance says the rate of change of mass inside the volume equals what flows in minus what flows out. Where it is used: at steady state the accumulation term vanishes and the balance collapses to the continuity equation, ṁ = ρ·A·v = constant along a stream — that is the form used at the piston in Step 3 and in the supply line in Step 5. The receiver is the opposite case: it is deliberately unsteady, so the accumulation term is the whole point — during a launch air only leaves, d(m)/dt = ṁin − ṁout with ṁin = 0, and Step 4 integrates that drain over the usable pressure drop; during recharge the same equation runs in reverse with ṁout = 0, which is Step 6.

Plain English: Count the air the way you'd count water in a bathtub — what accumulates is what ran in minus what drained out — and the tank, the piston, and the pipe are all just that one count.

Steady, one stream:  ṁ = ρ·A·v = constant   [continuity — Steps 3 & 5]

Symbolic results: ṁ = ρ·A·v (steady) · d(m)/dt = ṁin − ṁout (receiver)

Foundation B — Momentum balance: integral form, Cauchy, Navier–Stokes

The momentum balance says the sum of the forces on a control volume — pressure forces, gravity, wall shear, and any reaction force — equals the rate at which momentum accumulates inside it plus the net rate at which momentum flows out. Written differentially for a continuum, that statement is the Cauchy equation, with the stress divergence ∇·τ carrying the viscous forces. For a Newtonian fluid the stress is proportional to the velocity gradient (Foundation D's viscosity law, used in Step 7), and substituting it into Cauchy gives the Navier–Stokes equation. This project never solves Navier–Stokes directly; it uses what survives a stated list of simplifications. Steady (quasi-steady snapshot): the time derivative is dropped because each step is evaluated at one instant — the worst one — rather than tracked through the launch. One-dimensional: velocities are cross-section averages, so the momentum flux reduces to ṁ·v terms. Constant-area piston: the pressure force on the piston face reduces to p·A, with no area-change term. Negligible gravity in the line: the line is short and near-level and the pressure forces are orders of magnitude larger than the weight of the air in it, so ρg is dropped there (it is kept, as the train's weight normal to a level track, only in that it cancels out of the along-track balance). Viscous stress not solved pointwise: its net effect is carried by the friction factor f and the minor-loss sum ΣK in Step 5 instead. What remains after those drops is exactly the two momentum statements the design uses: ΣF = m·a on the train (Step 1) and F = p·A on the piston (Step 2).

Plain English: Newton's second law, written for flowing air, is a big equation — but once you freeze one instant, average across the pipe, and let the friction factor carry the rubbing, it shrinks to the two push equations this design actually uses.

ΣF = d/dt ∫CV vρ dV + ∫CS vρ(v·n) dA   [integral momentum balance]
ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + ∇·τ + ρg   [Cauchy equation]
ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = −∇p + μ∇²v + ρg   [Navier–Stokes, Newtonian fluid]
Simplified (steady snapshot, 1-D, constant area, ρg dropped in line):  ΣF = m·a  ·  F = p·A

Symbolic results: integral balance → Cauchy → Navier–Stokes → ΣF = m·a (Step 1) and F = p·A (Step 2)

Foundation C — Mechanical energy balance (engineering Bernoulli)

Dotting the momentum equation with the velocity gives the mechanical-energy balance: along a streamline, pressure energy, kinetic energy, and elevation head trade against each other, minus what friction destroys and plus what a machine adds. In head form, between a point in the receiver and a point at the cylinder, the compressor-work term is zero during the launch itself (the compressor did its work earlier, in Step 6) and the head loss hL is the Darcy–Weisbach plus minor-loss total of Step 5. Read in words: the pressure energy stored in the receiver becomes the kinetic energy of the moving air and then, through the piston, the kinetic energy of the train — minus the friction loss in the line and minus the work spent against drag. Elevation change is negligible on a level launch track, so the z terms cancel. This is the energy audit that Step 1's KE and work numbers and Step 5's ΔP must jointly satisfy.

Plain English: Energy is never created here, only converted — the tank's stored pressure turns into the train's speed, and whatever friction skims off along the way is simply gone.

Calculation — Foundation C (symbols only)

p₁/ρg + α₁v₁²/2g + z₁ + hmachine = p₂/ρg + α₂v₂²/2g + z₂ + hL   [mechanical energy, head form]
hL = [ f·(L/D) + ΣK ]·v²/2g   [head loss — Step 5]
Per unit mass:  Δp/ρ + Δ(v²/2) + gΔz = win − eloss   [win = compressor work, Step 6; Δz ≈ 0]

Symbolic result: stored pressure energy → train KE − line friction loss − drag work

Foundation D — Pressure: fluid statics, gauge vs. absolute

Fluid statics gives how pressure varies with depth, dp/dz = −ρg. Over the vertical extent of any component here that variation is tiny next to the cylinder pressure, so each component is treated as being at one uniform pressure — the static equation is checked, and then set aside, rather than ignored. The distinction that does matter is the zero of the scale. A gauge pressure is measured from the local atmosphere; an absolute pressure is measured from vacuum, and the two differ by exactly atmospheric pressure. The piston force balance (Step 2) correctly uses gauge pressure, because the atmosphere pushes on the back of the piston and on the outside of the train, so it cancels. The ideal-gas law (Steps 3, 4 and 6) must use absolute pressure: it counts the actual molecular density of the gas, and feeding it a gauge pressure would understate the density — and so the air mass — by the missing atmosphere.

Plain English: A tire gauge ignores the air pressing on everything around us — fine for forces, since that push cancels — but the gas law counts every molecule, so it has to start counting from a total vacuum.

Calculation — Foundation D (symbols only)

dp/dz = −ρg   [fluid statics — variation negligible across each component]
pabs = pgauge + patm
ρ = pabs/(R·T)   [ideal-gas law — absolute pressure only]

Symbolic results: pabs = pgauge + patm · gas law takes pabs, force balance takes pgauge

Step 1 — Train dynamics

The train is treated as a single point mass pushed along a straight, level track, so the governing statement is the linear momentum balance, ΣF = m·a: the net force on the train equals its mass m times its acceleration a. The launch is modelled with constant acceleration, starting from rest, so the speed builds linearly and the target speed v is reached at the end of the launch time t — that gives a = v / t directly, and the distance covered is the average speed (v/2) times t. Two energy statements follow from the same push: the kinetic energy the train must end up with, KE = ½·m·v², and the work the thrust does over the launch distance s, W = Fd·s. Power is force times speed, so it peaks at the end of the launch, where v is largest; the average power is the work divided by the launch time.

One allowance is added before the result is used anywhere else. Rolling resistance and air resistance are lumped into a single constant drag allowance, Fdrag, and the piston must deliver the momentum-balance thrust plus that allowance. That larger force is the design thrust, Fd — the quantity every later step is sized on. The allowance is not a guess: Step 8 calculates it from the standard drag equation for flow around a submerged body, evaluated at top speed and then held constant, which is conservative early in the launch when the train — and so the true drag — is slower.

Plain English: Shove a heavy train to highway speed in a few seconds — the push, the energy, and the power all follow from how heavy it is, how fast it must go, and how little time it gets.

Step 2 — Piston: pressure into thrust

Pressure is force spread over area, so the force balance on the piston face is simply F = p·A: the cylinder pressure p, acting over the whole piston area A, must produce exactly the design thrust from Step 1. The area follows from the piston diameter, A = π·D²/4, and the balance is then rearranged to solve for the pressure the cylinder must hold, p = Fd / A. Choosing D is the design trade here: a larger face needs less pressure for the same push, but sweeps more air per unit of travel in Step 3.

The pressure in this balance is gauge pressure, because the back of the piston faces the atmosphere — only the excess over atmospheric pressure does net work. The gas law in the next step needs absolute pressure, so atmospheric pressure is added back: pabs = pgauge + patm.

Plain English: Air pressure pressing on the whole piston face adds up to exactly the push the train needs — a bigger face needs less pressure for the same push.

A = π·D²/4  →  D = √(4A/π)

Symbolic results: A = Fd/pgauge · D = √(4A/π) · pabs = pgauge + patm

Step 3 — Air per launch

Air is compressible, so how much air a launch uses is a mass question, not just a volume question — and the ideal-gas law, pV = mRT, is what connects the two. As the piston travels the launch distance s it sweeps out a volume V = A·s. The density of the air filling that volume at cylinder pressure follows from the same law, ρ = pabs / (R·T), where R is the gas constant for air and T is the absolute temperature. Multiplying density by swept volume gives the mass of air per launch, mair.

The flows follow from the piston's motion. The volumetric flow is piston area times piston speed, Q = A·v, so it peaks at top speed; the mass flow is density times that, ṁ = ρ·Q. The average mass flow over the launch is mair / t. The peak flow — not the average — is the demanding case, and it is the flow the supply line in Step 5 must be sized for.

Plain English: One launch drinks a large mass of air, and near top speed it flows fastest — that peak flow, not the average, is what the pipe in Step 5 must survive.

Step 4 — Receiver sizing

The receiver is sized by a mass balance: the mass of air that leaves the tank during a launch must equal the drop in the mass stored inside it. Applying the ideal-gas law to the stored air at the charged pressure p1 and again at the end-of-launch pressure p2, the stored-mass drop is (p1 − p2)·Vtank / (R·T). Setting that drop equal to mair from Step 3 and rearranging gives the tank volume.

The key point is that the tank never empties. Only the usable drop (p1 − p2) delivers air, and p2 must stay above the cylinder's absolute pressure need from Step 2, or the piston could not be fed at the end of the launch. Sizing on p1 alone would understate the tank badly. The consequence — charge to a higher p1 and the same air mass fits in a smaller tank, at the cost of a higher pressure class — is quantified in the receiver tradeoff table in the worked example at the end of this section.

Plain English: The tank never empties — only the usable pressure drop does the delivering, so the tank is sized on that drop, not on its full pressure.

Symbolic result: Vtank = mair·R·T / (p1 − p2)

Step 5 — Supply-line sizing

Two statements govern the line. Continuity, in mass form ṁ = ρ·v·A, sets the air velocity in the pipe for a chosen diameter: at the peak mass flow from Step 3, a smaller area means a faster flow. The mechanical-energy balance then prices that velocity — the Darcy–Weisbach equation gives the friction loss, f·(L/D)·(ρv²/2), and each valve, bend, and manifold entry adds a minor loss, ΣK·(ρv²/2), where f is the Darcy friction factor from the Haaland correlation, L is the line length, D is the line diameter, μ is the air viscosity, and ΣK is the summed minor-loss coefficient. The line is evaluated at the line pressure, where the density ρline is taken from the ideal-gas law.

The model holds the density constant along the line, which is only defensible while the flow stays slow compared with the speed of sound — so the Mach number, M = v / c, is checked for every diameter, alongside the Reynolds number Re = ρ·v·D / μ, which confirms the flow is turbulent and sets f through Haaland. Because the losses scale with v² and v scales with 1/D², the total loss falls very steeply as the diameter grows — which is exactly what the diameter table in the worked example at the end of this section shows. In a short, fat line the minor losses, not wall friction, dominate the recommended size.

Plain English: In a wide enough pipe the air cruises slowly and loses only a small share of its pressure on the way — and in a short, fat line most of that loss is the valves and bends, not the pipe wall.

Step 5, continued — Where f comes from: Colebrook, Haaland, and the Moody chart

The friction factor is not a constant of the pipe; it is a function of the Reynolds number and the relative roughness ε/D, and the Moody chart is its graphical form: enter with Re on the horizontal axis, follow up to the curve for the pipe's ε/D, and read f off the left axis. At very high Re the curves flatten into the fully rough zone, where f depends on ε/D alone. Analytically the chart is the Colebrook equation — implicit, because f appears on both sides, so it is normally solved by iteration. The Haaland equation used in Step 5 is an explicit approximation of Colebrook, chosen so the demo can evaluate f directly with no iteration loop; the two agree closely at this design point, as the worked example confirms. Minor losses can be written two equivalent ways: as the ΣK sum used in Step 5, or as an equivalent length of extra straight pipe, Leq = K·D/f per fitting, folded into the Darcy–Weisbach length. The worked example gives the total equivalent length for this line — it is many times the physical length, which is the same statement as “minor losses dominate a short, fat line,” in length units.

Plain English: The Moody chart is a map from “how fast and how rough” to “how grippy” — Colebrook is that map written as an equation, Haaland is the same map in a form a calculator can answer in one shot, and the valves and bends can equally be counted as extra metres of pipe.

Step 6 — Compressor, recharge, expansion cooling

Between launches the compressor runs the Step 4 mass balance in reverse, slowly: it must replace mair kilograms of air, compressing them from atmospheric pressure up to the receiver pressure p1. Real compression is neither perfectly slow nor perfectly insulated, so it is modelled as a polytropic process with exponent n, between the isothermal limit and the adiabatic limit. The polytropic work formula gives the work per kilogram, w; multiplying by mair gives the energy per launch, and dividing by the allowed recharge time gives the compressor power. The two limits are evaluated as bounds in the worked example, so the design value can be seen sitting between them.

The same polytropic relation, run as an expansion from receiver pressure down to cylinder pressure, gives the temperature of the delivered air — expanding air cools, and the result is well below freezing, which is a real design constraint on valves and seals. The step closes with the normalised sensitivities, s(y; φ) = ∂ ln y / ∂ ln φ — the % change in an output per 1% change in an input — which identify which choices the design is most sensitive to: line diameter for pressure loss, and cylinder pressure for air mass. Their values appear in the worked example.

Plain English: A modest compressor refills the tank slowly between rides — the tank does the launching, not the compressor — but the expanding air chills hard, so the valves and seals have to work frozen.

Symbolic results: Ecomp = w·mair · Pcomp = Ecomp/trecharge · T2 = T1·(pabs/p1)(n−1)/n

Step 7 — Viscosity and flow regime: why this flow is turbulent

Newton's law of viscosity defines the fluid property behind every friction result on this page: the shear stress τ between layers of fluid is proportional to the velocity gradient across them, τ = μ·(du/dy), with μ the dynamic viscosity. How that stress organises the flow depends on the Reynolds number, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. At low Re the flow is laminar — smooth layers, a parabolic velocity profile, and the Hagen–Poiseuille law, in which pressure drop is proportional to velocity and f = 64/Re. At high Re the flow is turbulent — fluctuating and well mixed, with a much flatter velocity profile: almost all of the velocity change is squeezed into a thin boundary layer (viscous sublayer) at the wall while the core moves at nearly one speed, which is precisely why the one-dimensional, area-averaged model of Step 5 is legitimate. The line flow in this design sits far above the laminar–turbulent transition, so the flow is turbulent, Hagen–Poiseuille does not apply, and the pressure drop must come from Darcy–Weisbach with f from the Re- and roughness-dependent correlations of Step 5 — scaling with v², not with v.

Plain English: Slow, sticky flow glides in smooth layers and obeys the simple laminar law — this air is moving far too fast for that, so it tumbles turbulently, and the rougher turbulent rules are the ones that price the pipe.

Calculation — Step 7 (symbols only)

τ = μ·(du/dy)   [Newton's law of viscosity]
Re = ρ·v·D/μ   [inertial ÷ viscous forces]
Laminar only:  ΔP = 32·μ·L·v/D²  ·  f = 64/Re   [Hagen–Poiseuille — does not apply here]
Turbulent (this design):  ΔP from Darcy–Weisbach with f(Re, ε/D)   [Step 5]

Symbolic results: τ = μ·(du/dy) · regime set by Re · turbulent → Darcy–Weisbach, not Hagen–Poiseuille

Step 8 — Drag on the train: the allowance, calculated

The train moving through still air is the classic Transport 1 problem of flow around a submerged object: the air exerts a drag force made of pressure (form) drag on the train's blunt front and skin-friction drag along its sides, conventionally lumped into a single drag coefficient Cd multiplying the dynamic pressure of the oncoming flow and the frontal area Afront. Step 1's Fdrag is that force, evaluated at top speed — the worst instant — and then held constant across the launch as the design allowance. Holding the top-speed value is deliberately conservative: for most of the launch the train is slower and the true drag, growing with v², is smaller, so the piston is sized for slightly more resistance than it will actually meet. The aerodynamic part dominates the allowance; rolling resistance of the wheels is small beside it at these speeds and is absorbed in the rounding of the result, as the worked example shows.

Plain English: Pushing a blunt train through air at highway speed takes a real, calculable shove of its own — the drag formula prices that shove, and it lands right where the allowance already sat.

Calculation — Step 8 (symbols only)

Fdrag = ½·ρair·v²·Cd·Afront   [drag on a submerged body]
Fd = m·a + Fdrag   [feeds Step 1 — design thrust unchanged]

Symbolic result: Fdrag = ½·ρair·v²·Cd·Afront

Step 9 — Compressible flow: choked-flow check at the launch valve

One compressible-flow check remains, at the launch valve between the receiver and the cylinder. For flow from a stagnation reservoir (pressure p0, temperature T0) through an opening, the mass flux rises as the downstream pressure falls — until the throat reaches the local speed of sound. Below a critical downstream-to-upstream pressure ratio, set only by the ratio of specific heats γ, the flow is choked: the flux is capped at the value G* fixed by the upstream state alone, and lowering the downstream pressure further cannot pass more air. The design check is a comparison of two ratios: the critical ratio from the isentropic relations, and the actual cylinder-to-receiver pressure ratio during the launch. Because the actual ratio sits below the critical one, the valve runs choked, and the valve area needed to pass the peak mass flow of Step 3 follows directly from ṁpeak = G*·Avalve. Choking here is not a failure — it is what meters the launch — but it means the valve, sized by this step, and not the cylinder pressure, sets the maximum delivery rate.

Plain English: Open a high-pressure tank and the air can only escape so fast — once the exit hits the speed of sound the flow is capped no matter how low the pressure beyond it falls — and that cap is what sizes the launch valve.

Symbolic results: choked when pcyl/p0 < (2/(γ+1))γ/(γ−1) · Avalve = ṁpeak/G*

Step 10 — Dimensional analysis: the Pi groups that run the design

The last step steps back from the algebra. The line-and-valve problem involves the variables Δp, ρ, v, D, μ, ε, L and the reservoir and cylinder pressures, expressed in three fundamental dimensions (mass, length, time). The Buckingham-Pi theorem then guarantees the behaviour is fully described by a small set of independent dimensionless groups — and they are exactly the groups the earlier steps kept computing. Each one controls a distinct design question. Re sets the flow regime and, with ε/D, the friction factor (Steps 5 and 7). Mach M decides whether the constant-density line model is legitimate (Step 5). The Euler number Eu = Δp/(ρ·v²) is the pressure loss measured in units of the flow's own dynamic pressure — comparing it with the loss bracket of Step 5 shows it is that bracket, halved. L/D weighs wall friction against the fittings (Step 5). ε/D fixes the roughness regime on the Moody chart (Step 5, continued). The pressure ratios — end-to-start in the receiver, and cylinder-to-receiver at the valve — set the tank size (Step 4) and the choking verdict (Step 9). Two launches with the same Pi groups behave identically when scaled, which is why the Maxx Force comparison in Section 4 is made in these terms — same speed and pressure class, different acceleration group — rather than by comparing hardware nobody publishes.

Plain English: Strip the units off the problem and a handful of pure numbers remain — match those numbers and any two launches, at any size, behave the same way; they are the real dials of the design.

Symbolic results: Re (regime, f) · M (compressibility) · Eu (loss) · L/D (friction share) · ε/D (roughness) · pressure ratios (tank, choking)

Assumptions & limits — stated explicitly

Every result in this section rests on the assumptions below. Each is listed with why it is reasonable here and where it breaks down.

  • Steady / quasi-steady snapshots: each step is evaluated at a single worst instant (peak flow, lowest receiver pressure) instead of integrated through the launch. Reasonable because the flow in the line adjusts far faster than the train accelerates; it breaks down in the first fraction of a second while the valve is still opening.
  • Ideal gas: air is treated with pV = mRT throughout. Reasonable at these pressures near ambient temperature, where the compressibility factor stays close to unity; it breaks down at much higher pressures or at the cold expanded temperatures of Step 6, where a Z-factor correction would shift the density slightly.
  • Isothermal receiver: the stored air is taken to stay at one temperature while it drains. Reasonable for sizing the mass from the two end pressures, since the tank's metal mass buffers the change; it breaks down in detail — the real tank cools as it discharges, delivering marginally less mass than the isothermal count.
  • Negligible elevation change: the track and line are treated as level, so no hydrostatic or elevation-head term appears. Reasonable because any height difference is metres at most against megapascal pressures; it would break down only for a launch routed over a large vertical rise.
  • Constant piston pressure: the valve is assumed to hold the cylinder at one pressure for the whole launch. Reasonable because the receiver's end pressure is specified above the cylinder's need, leaving margin for the sag; it breaks down at the very end of the launch if that margin is spent, which is exactly the sizing point the model notes below examine.
  • Constant line density: the supply line is evaluated at one density, taken at the line pressure. Reasonable while the Mach number stays low (Step 5 checks it for every diameter); it breaks down for small diameters, where the Mach number climbs and the density genuinely varies along the line.
  • Constant drag at its top-speed value: the Step 8 drag is evaluated once, at top speed, and held. Reasonable — and conservative — because the true drag grows with v² and is smaller for most of the launch; it breaks down as a prediction of the instantaneous force, though not as a sizing allowance.
  • Expansion cooling not fed back: the Step 6 temperature drop is computed and reported but not looped back into the densities of Steps 3–5. Reasonable because colder air is denser, so the un-corrected model overstates, not understates, the volumetric demand; it breaks down for the hardware questions — icing, seal materials — where the cold itself is the design driver.

Notes · Model levels, method, sizing point

  • Two levels of model — what the shortcuts cost: sizing the receiver on p1 alone instead of the usable drop (p1 − p2) understates the tank; a friction-only line loss understates ΔP because minor losses dominate this short line; isothermal compression understates the recharge energy against the polytropic model. Each shortcut's numeric cost is quantified in the worked example below.
  • How the page solves it: the model is algebraic — no ODE to integrate. The demo runs Steps 1–6 in order, each step consuming the previous output; Haaland is explicit (unlike Colebrook it needs no iteration), and the balances are evaluated quasi-steadily, at the worst snapshot.
  • Locating the sizing point: everything is sized at the end of the launch, where ṁ peaks just as receiver pressure bottoms out at p2, leaving only the margin (p2 − pabs) above the cylinder's need. Sizing at ṁavg would understate line losses several-fold, since losses scale with ṁ².

Step 7 — regime:  Re = 12.2 × 10⁶ ≫ 4,000 → fully turbulent. Hagen–Poiseuille (laminar, f = 64/Re) does not apply; Darcy–Weisbach with f(Re, ε/D) is the correct law, as used in Step 5.
Step 8 — drag:  Fdrag = ½·ρair·v²·Cd·Afront = ½ × 1.2 × 35² × 1.1 × 6.25 = 5.05 kN ≈ 5.0 kN — the calculated drag lands on the 5 kN allowance, so the design thrust stands: Fd = 93.8 + 5 = 98.8 kN (unchanged).
Step 9 — choked flow:  p*/p0 = (2/(γ+1))γ/(γ−1) = 0.528 for air (γ = 1.4). Actual ratio pcyl/p0 = 1.07 / 3.0 = 0.357 < 0.528 → the launch valve is choked.
G* = 7,001 kg/m²/s  (p0 = 3.0 MPa abs, T0 = 300 K, R = 287 J/kg·K)  →  Avalve = ṁpeak/G* = 44.3 / 7,001 = 0.00633 m²  →  Dvalve = √(4A/π) = 0.090 m (90 mm).

Equivalent length of the fittings: Leq = ΣK·D/f = 6 × 0.25 / 0.0136 = 110 m of extra pipe, against 15 m of physical line — minor losses dominate, in length units.
Step 10 — Euler number:  Eu = Δp/(ρ·v²) = 120 kPa / (23.2 × 38.9²) = 3.42 = ½·[f·(L/D) + ΣK] = ½ × [0.0136 × 60 + 6] — the dimensionless loss agrees with the Step 5 bracket.

Section 8 · Reflection

What I learned, and what I want to learn next

In plain English

The three big takeaways, no jargon: (1) Storing energy up and releasing it in a burst beats buying a bigger motor — every time. (2) Pipe size matters way more than you'd guess: make a pipe just a little wider and the wasted pressure drops dramatically — and in a short fat pipe, the valves and bends waste more than the pipe itself. (3) Air is not just “invisible stuff”: it has weight (62 kg per launch!), it gets freezing cold when it expands, and if you push it through too small a pipe it starts behaving like it's approaching the speed of sound. The boxes below say the same things in course language, plus what I'd study next — mainly, tracking the launch second-by-second instead of in snapshots, and designing for the cold.

What I learned

  • Energy storage decouples average from peak power. The receiver — not the compressor — is the launch. That idea (charge slowly, discharge fast) generalizes to regenerative braking, grid storage, and compressed-air energy storage.
  • Pressure loss is brutally sensitive to diameter (sensitivity ≈ −4.14 here, between the D⁻⁴ minor-loss and D⁻⁵ friction laws). "Slightly bigger pipe" is often the cheapest power a designer can buy — and in a short line, the fittings matter more than the pipe.
  • A compressible working fluid changes every step: density follows pressure through the ideal-gas law, the flow has to be checked against the speed of sound (Mach), and the mass balance — not a volume balance — is what sizes the tank.
  • Where you evaluate a calculation matters: sizing the line at peak mass flow instead of average mass flow is the difference between a design that works on paper and one that works at second 2.7.
  • AI workflow: the plan improved most when it was forced to state its assumptions and name its own weak points in advance — which is exactly how the two-model critique in Section 6 is structured, and why its accept/reject judgment has to stay with the engineer, not the models.

What I want to learn next

  • Transient modeling: time-stepping receiver pressure, temperature, flow, and train acceleration together instead of quasi-steady snapshots.
  • Designing for the cold: air drying, icing in fast valves, and seal materials at −36 °C.
  • Real component data: reading manufacturer compressor maps and valve flow coefficients (Cv) instead of assumed ΣK values.
  • Compressibility corrections: when the ideal-gas law stops being good enough, and how real-gas (Z-factor) sizing changes the receiver and compressor numbers.
  • Why parks choose among launch technologies: compressed air vs. magnetic (LSM/LIM) launches — efficiency, maintenance, and ride feel.
